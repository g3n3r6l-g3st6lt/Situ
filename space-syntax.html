What space syntax is

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 9, 2026

What it does

Space syntax describes where each street sits in the whole street network. It asks two questions about every street: how easy it is to reach from everywhere else, and how often it lies on the way between other places.

It looks at the shape of the network and nothing else. Shops and people are outside its view. I find that limit useful, because it lets me ask how much of Griffintown’s movement the layout alone can explain.

Where it comes from

Bill Hillier and Julienne Hanson started the method at University College London. Their book The Social Logic of Space (1984) argued that the arrangement of spaces carries social meaning and can be measured.

Year Work What it added
1993 Hillier, Penn, Hanson, Grajewski & Xu “Natural movement”: the shape of the network is a main driver of how many people walk each street.
2004 Ratti A critique: results depend on how the map is drawn, and early methods ignored metres.
2005 Hillier & Iida Counting turns matched observed movement better than counting metres.
2007 Turner Ordinary street centrelines can be analysed, so I can use the city’s own street file.

Full references are on the Resources page.

NoteHow settled is it?

The natural movement claim is influential and still debated. Its strength varies from place to place and depends on land use. In the 1993 paper itself, the correlation between integration and movement across ten parts of King’s Cross ranged from 0.25 to 0.80, and shops could outweigh the layout (Hillier et al. 1993, pp. 31, 46). Ratti (2004, p. 492) adds that the method leaves land use out. So in Situ I test the claim in Griffintown. I do not assume it.

Segments and depth

A segment is a piece of street between two junctions, the same unit as in S1 Streets. Depth is the distance between two segments. The made-up town below counts one step for each change of street, so it can be checked by hand. The real analysis counts angles: a right angle costs 1 and a 45° turn costs 0.5 (Turner 2007, p. 542). People seem to judge a route by how much it turns more than by its length (Hillier & Iida 2005).

Integration and choice

Integration measures how close a segment is, on average, to every other segment. A segment with high integration is easy to get to. Network science calls this closeness centrality (Sabidussi 1966).

Choice imagines the shortest route between every pair of segments and counts how many of those routes pass through each one. A segment with high choice is often passed through. Network science calls this betweenness centrality (Freeman 1977).

Radius

The analysis can be limited to a distance around each segment, called the radius. At 400 m it asks how central a street is within about five minutes’ walk. Radius n has no limit. In the literature, 400 to 800 m is the usual low radius and 4,000 to 8,000 m the usual high one (van Nes & Yamu 2021, p. 65).

My analysis (Sample 4) uses 200 to 800 m and radius n for pedestrians, and 1200 and 2000 m for vehicles. A street can be central at 400 m and peripheral at radius n, and that contrast often tells me more than any single value.

A made-up town

I invented this town so the numbers can be checked by hand. It has a main street (M1 to M5), cross streets to the north and south, a back street (B1, B2) and a cul-de-sac (C1, C2).

  • Integration
  • Choice

Starting from M1 at depth 0, every street one step away has depth 1, the next ones depth 2, and so on. C2, at the end of the cul-de-sac, is six steps away.

M3 has the highest integration (0.55), because it sits in the middle. C2 has the lowest (0.24), because every other street is far from it.

M4 has the highest choice (55), because routes from the west side to the east side and to the cul-de-sac must cross it. C1 has 15, since every route to C2 passes through it. A dead end like C2 scores 0.

TipWhat the toy shows

M3 is the best place to be, and M4 is the best place to pass through. The two measures differ, so I keep both.

The values in the results

The results also give normalised forms, NAIN for integration and NACH for choice, that can be compared between networks of different sizes (Hillier, Yang & Turner 2012, pp. 160, 165 and 191). NACH also evens out raw choice, where a few streets score very high and most score low. The paper gives two slightly different versions of NAIN, so I compare numbers only within one version (Checking the readings).

What it leaves out

Left out In Griffintown
what is on the street a metro exit, a grocery store
height and level stairs, the canal banks
quality of the walk sidewalk width, trees, noise, snow
time morning and evening, summer and winter
traffic rules one-way streets, closed crossings
who is walking age and purpose

In Situ each of these belongs to another system, such as S3 Rules or S5 Rhythm. That keeps space syntax clean as a measure.
