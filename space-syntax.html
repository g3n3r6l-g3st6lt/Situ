What space syntax is

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 15, 2026

Where it comes from

Space syntax began with Bill Hillier and Julienne Hanson at University College London. Their book The Social Logic of Space (1984) argued that the way spaces are arranged carries social meaning, and that it can be measured. In a city, the method asks two questions about every street: how easy it is to reach from everywhere else, and how often it lies on the way between other places.

It looks at the shape of the network and nothing else. I find that limit useful. It lets me ask how much of Griffintown’s movement the layout alone can explain.

Segments and depth

The unit is the segment: a piece of street between two junctions, as in S1 Streets. Depth is the distance between two segments, counted by how much you turn. A right angle costs 1 and a 45° turn costs 0.5 (Turner 2007, p. 542). Hillier and Iida (2005) found that counting turns matched observed movement better than counting metres. Turner also showed that ordinary street centrelines can be analysed this way, so I can use the city’s own street file.

Integration and choice

Integration measures how close a segment is, on average, to every other segment. A street with high integration is easy to get to. Network science calls this closeness centrality (Sabidussi 1966).

Choice imagines the shortest route between every pair of segments and counts how many of those routes pass through each one. A street with high choice is often passed through. Network science calls this betweenness centrality (Freeman 1977).

The results also include normalised versions (Hillier, Yang & Turner 2012, pp. 160, 165 and 191). NAIN rescales integration so that networks of different sizes can be compared. NACH does the same for choice. It also evens out a skew in choice, where a few streets score very high and most score low. The paper gives two versions of NAIN, so I compare NAIN numbers only within one version (Reading closely).

Natural movement

Hillier, Penn, Hanson, Grajewski and Xu (1993) called their central claim “natural movement”. It says the shape of the network is a main driver of how many people walk each street. The full references are in Resources.

NoteStill debated

The claim is influential, and its strength varies with place and land use. In the 1993 paper itself, the correlation between integration and movement across ten parts of King’s Cross ranged from 0.25 to 0.80. Shops could also multiply movement beyond what the layout predicts (Hillier et al. 1993, pp. 31, 46). Ratti (2004, p. 492) adds that results depend on how the map is drawn, and that the method leaves land use out. So in Situ the claim is something I test in Griffintown.

Radius

The analysis can be limited to a distance around each segment, called the radius. At 400 m it asks how central a street is within about five minutes’ walk. Radius n has no limit, which here means the whole island. Studies usually take 400 to 800 m as the low radius and 4,000 to 8,000 m as the high one (van Nes & Yamu 2021, p. 65). For walking I use 200, 400 and 800 m and radius n. For driving I use 1200 and 2000 m. A street can be central at 400 m and remote at radius n. That contrast often tells me more than any single value.

A made-up town

I invented this town so the numbers can be checked by hand. Here depth counts one step per change of street. The town has a main street (M1 to M5), cross streets to the north and south, a back street (B1, B2) and a cul-de-sac (C1, C2).

  • Depth
  • Integration
  • Choice

Start from M1 at depth 0. Every street one step away has depth 1, the next ones depth 2, and so on. C2, at the end of the cul-de-sac, is six steps away.

M3 has the highest integration (0.55), because it sits in the middle. C2 has the lowest (0.24), because every other street is far from it.

M4 has the highest choice (55). Routes from the west side to the east side and to the cul-de-sac must all cross it. C1 has 15, since every route to C2 passes through it. A dead end like C2 scores 0.

TipWhat the toy shows

M3 is the best place to be, and M4 is the best place to pass through. The two measures differ, so I keep both.

What it leaves out

Left out In Griffintown
what is on the street a metro exit, a grocery store
height and level stairs, the canal banks
quality of the walk sidewalk width, trees, noise, snow
time morning and evening, summer and winter
traffic rules one-way streets, closed crossings
who is walking age and purpose

In Situ each of these belongs to another system, such as S3 Rules or S5 Rhythm. That keeps space syntax clean enough to serve as a measuring stick for the rest.

Why I use it

A measuring stick

My simulated walkers (agents) never read space syntax values. I compare their movement with the values afterwards, so the comparison is a real test. If the agents preferred high-scoring streets, the test would be circular: the answer would go in and come back out.

Choice is already a route model. It is built on the route with the least turning between every pair of segments (Turner 2007, p. 544). Walkers who took those routes, spread evenly over all pairs, would match choice by construction, and the match would prove nothing. So the walkers S9 imagines pick routes for their own reasons, such as where they are going and the time of day. Space syntax enters Situ only at the end, in S9 Observer.

What the comparison can show

  • Simulation
  • Real counts
  • Players

Where the walkers and choice agree, the layout alone may explain the movement. Where they disagree, something else is at work, such as a building or a rule.

The city’s movement counts check the model against reality, and they are thin: seven S1 junctions, one to three days each, between February 2024 and May 2026. They are enough to raise a warning and too few to prove anything.

In the game I can watch whether players drift toward well-integrated streets, the ones that are easy to reach. That bears on my research question about wayfinding.

Why this method

It runs in QGIS on the city’s open street file, and the same file with the same settings gives the same values. Forty years of studies use it, so I can set Griffintown beside other cities. It was built to study how layout and social life relate, which is the subject of my research. Its blind spots are well documented, and each one is handled by another Situ system.

What this means for my data

Values belong to the network they were calculated on. A missing path makes its neighbours look less connected than they are. So I added the city’s off-street paths before the analysis (The walking network).

The edge of the map also distorts. Streets near it look remote only because the map stops there (Ratti 2004, p. 496; van Nes & Yamu 2021, p. 51). So the analysis covers the whole island, and I cut Griffintown out of the results afterwards.
