S9 Observer

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 17, 2026

What S9 does

S9 is the mother-analyzer. The systems before it hold the open data I could find from my desk, with as few assumptions as I could manage. S9 is where I imagine: it combines them into flows of people, and only then compares those flows with what the city counted and with space syntax.

Imagining needs rules. S9 writes each one in a ledger with its source, or marks it as an assumption. It builds rival readings, each changing one rule, so a difference between them points at that rule. No reading is tuned to the counts or space syntax: a test scrambles both and nothing moves.

S9 grows in slices. The first is a weekday morning on foot.

The first slice: a weekday morning on foot

Between 6 and 10 h, S9 sends four kinds of walker across the S1 streets. They leave the area through its “doors”, where streets cross its edge.

Who From To People
residents who walk to work outside the area home (S7) Place Ville Marie, through the door that makes the walk shortest 1,134
residents who walk to work inside it home a building with business premises (S4) 262
residents who ride home the nearest metro entrance or bus stop 1,069
workers who ride in the entrance or stop nearest their workplace the workplace 1,067

That makes about 3,500 walks, about 1,500 of them between 8 and 9 h. The reading shortest routes them by the shortest walk. Two others change that rule:

  • open: enclosed streets feel longer, by up to half, using the area seen from S8.
  • straight: the fewest degrees of turning, which Hillier and Iida (2005) found fits observed movement in London better than the shortest route.

What it found

commuters make up about a quarter of the pedestrians counted at 7 and 8 h · the busiest counted junction gets none of them

At the seven counted junctions, S9’s commuters make up 22 to 27 % of the pedestrians counted, whichever reading routes them (certain, for these rules). The rest are people this slice does not hold: errands, school runs, dog walks, visitors and people crossing from beyond the area. The counts give the size of that gap, and later slices will try to fill it.

The busiest counted junction, Robert-Bourassa at Saint-Jacques, saw 868 pedestrians at 8 h. No S9 commuter passes there, in any reading or stress test. It lies just north of the area, beyond the doors S9’s walkers take towards downtown and the metro. Its walkers come from outside this slice, probably from Old Montréal or from beyond the area to the east (guessing). It is the clearest question this slice raises, and it is listed as a checkpoint.

Where commuters do pass, they explain more: half to three quarters of the count at Guy and Ottawa (the one junction counted on three days), and a third to a half at de la Montagne and Notre-Dame, on the road to Lucien-L’Allier and Bonaventure.

The four Wellington junctions were counted on the same February morning. Their counts are even, from 221 to 303 pedestrians at 8 h. The shortest routes crowd the commuters onto Rue Young (164) and leave Rue du Shannon almost empty (18), a ninefold spread. The fewest-turns routes spread them out to a threefold one. Four junctions on one day settle nothing, so I read this as a lead: walkers here may use the parallel streets more evenly than the shortest walk predicts (likely, as a lead).

“Open” changes the route of 13 % of walkers, mostly from Rue Young to the more open Rue Peel, and adds three metres to the median walk. “Straight” changes 34 % and sends some on long detours: 15 % walk more than a quarter further than needed. In a grid this tight, the view matters little to a commuter’s route, and counting turns alone sends walkers further than people would plausibly accept.

Walking choice, in space syntax, is how often a street lies on the way between other places. The morning’s flows agree with it only weakly: rank correlation 0.24 for the shortest routes and 0.29 for the fewest turns, where 1 means the same order (certain, as computed). Choice counts every route between every pair of streets. The morning sends everyone to a few doors and stations, so flows bunch on Peel and de la Montagne where choice is spread out.

My reading, and it is an interpretation: in the morning, Griffintown’s streets carry commuters mostly as a way out, towards the stations and downtown. Most of the pedestrians counted are doing something else.

Beyond the doors: the ring

S1 has no streets beyond the doors. To see where walkers go next, S9 draws a straight line from each one’s door to where it is heading, and counts it at one of the counted intersections of S5’s ring around the area when the line passes within 40 m (assumption A13). Before reading a single ring count, I wrote down what I expected:

Expected before reading the ring What the ring showed
R1 Commuters reach the ring on its north-west side, at Saint-Jacques with Guy and Lucien-L’Allier partly: 40 at Lucien-L’Allier against 325 counted at 8 h, but only 3 at Guy
R2 The sites towards Old Montréal and across the canal get no commuters, or almost none yes: none at all
R3 Where the ring does get commuters, they explain a smaller share than inside yes: 0.5 % of the ring’s count at 8 h, against 22 % inside
R4 If many people walk between Griffintown and Old Montréal, the McGill sites will show large counts that S9 leaves near zero the counts are large (2,148 at McGill and Notre-Dame), but Old Montréal’s offices would fill them without anyone from Griffintown, so the ring cannot settle this

The clearest sign is on the east edge. Just past the area towards the Cité du Multimédia, de la Commune at Mill and Brennan at Prince each counted about 120 pedestrians at 8 h on two weekdays in May 2026. S9 sends nobody that way. With the empty Robert-Bourassa junction, this points to movement between Griffintown and the east that this slice does not hold (likely, as a lead). Across the canal, the counts are small and S9 is empty too. The canal may act as an edge in the morning (guessing).

What it rests on

Everything above stands on the ledger: five pieces of evidence and thirteen assumptions. The ones that move the results most are where walkers to work outside the area go (A01), how many resident walkers work inside it (A02) and which stop riders choose (A04). The stress tests vary each of them.

The counts are thin: seven junctions, six counted on a single day, four of them on Wellington on the same day in February 2024. They show where the imagined morning falls short. They cannot rank the readings, and S9 does not try.

Checkpoints

Two. People arrive to work in the small part of census tract 4620071.00 (an area the census reports on) inside the area, yet S4 lists no building with business premises there. And nobody in S9 walks through Robert-Bourassa at Saint-Jacques, the busiest junction counted.
