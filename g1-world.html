G1 World

The first step from the systems to a game: the built world

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 22, 2026

Asks What can a game stand on and move among, taken from the systems with nothing made up?

Found The streets and 296 building masses in one frame, with 2,783 props. 284 heights are measured, most from Québec’s 2023 lidar, and 131 roofs come from the city’s 2016 3D buildings. 6 buildings have no height in any source, and stay without one.

Stands verified: 11 tests pass. 39 decisions in the ledger, 5 of them assumptions.

Leaves out Facades, interiors and most entrances, and the roofs of 165 buildings: 145 the 2016 model does not include, and 20 whose 2016 roof no longer fits their height. A game that shows them invents them.

In the game World: the ground and the masses (From systems to a game).

Trace ledger · run record · uses S1, S4 and S6

What G1 does

G1 reads the outputs of S1, S4 and S6, and writes them as files a game engine can load. S1 gives the ground. S4 gives the building masses, and S6 the objects and sidewalks. It does not choose an engine. Every element keeps the id of the row it came from, so a block in a game can be traced back to its building in S4 and from there to its source.

The frame

All coordinates are metres from one origin: the study area’s south-west corner, rounded down to 10 m, at (299180, 5038120) in the map projection the systems use (EPSG:32188). x points east, y north, z up. True north is kept, so the sun can be placed later. An engine converts the units and the axes it needs (W01, W02).

Heights

Since G1b, most heights are measured. Since 2026-10-29 the first source is Québec’s 2023 lidar (W32): its ground and building points, minus its 2023 bare-ground model, give each 1 m cell’s height above ground. The city’s 2015 surface model minus its 2015 terrain does the same for 2015. A building’s height is the 90th percentile of those heights inside its outline, leaving out a 1 m band along the edge (W16). S4 measures both and sets them beside the other sources (S4: heights, by source).

The lidar flew on 2023-11-21. For the 5 buildings the tax roll dates 2023 or later, it is used when their newest other height (OpenStreetMap, else storeys) lies within one storey of it: 3 buildings. For the other 2 it is discarded, since it likely shows the building before or one half built (W33). Every other source more than one storey from the height chosen is discarded from the building too, with how far and why (W35): 43 heights on 31 buildings. One outline can hold a podium and a tower. Where the 2023 lidar measures the outline’s parts and their heights differ by more than one storey, the building is drawn as its outline at a base height with each part standing on it at its own height (W36): 15 buildings from parts mapped in OpenStreetMap, 3 from the 2016 model’s parts. District Griffin sur Peel, for instance, was one 59 m mass; it is now a 20.3 m base with towers of 60.1 and 59.5 m. Where no parts are mapped, the 2023 lidar’s own height zones split the outline (W38): its cells are sorted into levels more than one storey apart, and each level above the lowest becomes zones outlined by their 1 m cells, smoothed by 1 m: 40 buildings, 44 zones. Their edges come from the lidar’s cells, not from a survey of the walls. Where neither parts nor zones split an outline and a quarter of it stands more than one storey below the height drawn, it stays one mass and a checkpoint asks what it holds (W37): 12 buildings. S4 keeps them all (evidence). Before 2026-10-29, the 2015 surface came first. It cannot measure a building that went up later. On 61 outlines it shows no building at all, and for 17 more the tax roll dates the building 2015 or later, so what the surface shows there is the building before (W15, W17). Those buildings take, in this order, a height from the 2016 3D buildings (Roofs), an OpenStreetMap height, or the storey rule.

The two measurements agree. Where a building has both a 2015 surface height and an OpenStreetMap height, the median difference is 0.37 m and the median absolute difference 0.89 m, over 14 buildings (test report).

The first storey rule was too low. G1 first used 3.33 m per storey, the median of 27 mostly tall buildings. Measured against the 2015 surface, that rule came out 1.16 m too low on the median building, and 1.99 m off in absolute terms (143 buildings). The rule then used a height per storey measured on 157 buildings by the 2015 surface (all uses 3.75 m). Since 2026-10-29 it reads the storey S4 measures with the 2023 lidar, the same storey S4 uses to say whether two heights agree (W05):

Use Height per storey Measured buildings
residential 3.39 m 135
commerce 4.27 m 28
services 3.90 m 25

any other use 3.55 m (all uses) 210

The 48 buildings whose height comes from storeys came out 0.2 to 1.9 m lower than with the 2015 storey.

The sources are in two slightly different datums (W18). A search for the best shift between them, within 2 m, found none: the outlines and the surface agree as they are.

Roofs

The city’s 2016 3D buildings model each building part with its roof faces. Each part goes to the outline its ground overlaps most, when at least half of its ground lies there (W23); until 2026-10-29 a part went to the outline holding the centre of its ground, and 11 parts reached mostly past their outline. 197 parts fall on 151 outlines. A 2016 roof stays on a building only when its roof top lies within one storey (3.5 m) of the building’s height (W34): 131 roofs stay, with 11,500 faces kept in roofs_2016.geojson as 3D faces in the frame, and 20 are set aside, each with its reason in the block (W35). A face sloping less than 5 degrees is flat (W22): 99 kept roofs are flat, 28 mixed and 4 pitched. The other 145 outlines have no part in the 2016 model, most of them buildings finished after 2016. Their roofs, and the 20 set aside, stay unknown (W14).

A finding about the 2016 model. Its own height field, measuredHeight, is 4.4 m more than its roof tops stand above the 2015 terrain. Its roof tops, though, sit exactly where the 2015 surface puts them. So measuredHeight counts from a ground set lower than the street. Situ takes the 2016 roof top and subtracts the 2015 terrain (W25). Measured that way, the 2016 model agrees with the 2015 surface to a median of 0.23 m on 131 buildings, and it gives a height to 6 buildings the 2015 surface could not measure. No shift between the model and the outlines was found within 1 m (W24).

Ground

The ground is the city’s 2015 terrain model, written as a 1 m grid over everything G1 places (W13). Every street vertex, junction and prop takes its height from it, in metres above the frame’s z origin, the lowest ground in the grid (3 m above sea, W19). The terrain model stops at the edge of the Sud-Ouest borough, so 16 street elements north of it have no height, and say so.

Each building is a separate structure set on that ground (W20). Its base is the lowest ground along its outline, so no wall floats; its top is the median ground along the outline plus its height. On 10 outlines the ground drops more than 3 m below its median, at a ramp, the canal edge or an outline that is off; each is a checkpoint.

Doors and public use

No source used here records where a building’s entrance is. G1 puts a door at the point of the outline closest to the building’s access street from S4 (W07, assumption). One building has no walkable street within reach, so it has no door. For one more, S1’s Rue Barré runs through the outline, so the street file or the outline is out of date (a checkpoint).

Each block also says what S4 records of a public use (W08, assumption). This marks a building where a player could act. It does not say a door is open.

Props

S6 lists some objects twice: once from OpenStreetMap and once from Mapillary’s photo detection. G1 keeps every mapped object at its mapped position and drops a detection that S6 matched to one (W09). 2,898 S6 rows become 2,783 props. In 26 places the match is not one to one, such as one detection near two mapped crossings, and the count there is unsure. G1 lists them as checkpoints. 34 props were found only in photos, in no photo since 2023, and are marked possibly gone (W11).

Tests

Eleven checks, all PASS (report). The ground is exactly S1’s segments and junctions, once each, and moving it back by the origin gives S1’s coordinates to the centimetre. Every height is recomputed from S4 and the 2015 measurements in the order the ledger gives, and the 15 without a source stay empty. Every door sits on its outline, no further from its street than S4 measured. Every S6 row is accounted for, either as a prop or as a detection matched to one. Every height in the frame is read again from the terrain picture by a separate sampler, and no outline corner lies below its building’s base. The ledger covers every layer, and the files keep the published format: a version number, numbers stored as numbers, and missing values stored as empty (null), never as zero.

The door check failed at first. One building’s door came out 4.9 m from the street, where S4 had 1.1 m. Rue Barré runs through that outline, and the first door rule only looked at corners. The rule now places the door where the street enters the outline, and the building is a checkpoint.

Plan: G1b, the world completed

G1b fills empty fields from measurements, and replaces the storey rule wherever a measured height exists. Two city sources measure buildings: the 2015 surface and terrain models (downloaded), and the 2016 3D buildings, which also give roof shapes. Both are older than many of Griffintown’s towers, so each building says which source, if any, saw it.
