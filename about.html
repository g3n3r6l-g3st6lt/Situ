About Situ

A process book on urban rhythm and spatial culture in Griffintown, Montréal

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 22, 2026

What Situ is

Situ is the prototype for my PhD research-creation project. It asks whether a game-like 3D experience can pass on and archive the spatial culture and urban rhythm of Griffintown, Montréal. I look at two eras: today, and the 1920s to 1940s. I work from the city’s open data and from my own observation, and I read both through urban theory. Game design then carries the result to a player.

Nothing in Situ archives anything yet. What exists so far is a foundation: the street network with its rules and buildings, and the measures I will test it against.

Research-creation: systems made for a game

Situ is research-creation: the research and the making answer each other. The research half is the systems. They hold what open data and photos say about Griffintown, with every source and gap in view, and each is tested before the next one uses it. The making half is the game, which is planned.

The systems are made to inform the game’s design. Each one prepares a part of Griffintown a game needs: the ground one can move on (S1), how that ground is laid out (S2), the rules of the road (S3), places to go (S4), the hours (S5), the objects in the street (S6), who commutes and when (S7), what can be seen (S8), and movement imagined from all of these (S9). Designing the game means reading each system as a source and translating it into rules and spaces. Playing it sends something back to the research: routes to compare with the counts and with space syntax, and questions to bring to residents.

From systems to play sets out, system by system, what each could give the game and what it cannot. Until the game exists, every one of those questions is planned.

The idea

Spatial culture

Hillier described a spatial culture as “a distinctive way of ordering space” (Hillier 1989, quoted in Griffiths & von Lünen 2016, p. xxiii). Griffiths and von Lünen see the field as the link between how a built place works and what it comes to mean to the people who live there (2016, p. xxi). Adams (2024, introduction) wants spatial culture to be “qualified and documented”. That is close to what an archive needs.

Rhythm

A street layout holds no time. Space syntax is the method Hillier and Hanson started for measuring how a street network is arranged (What space syntax is). It tends to strip social practice of its time (Griffiths & von Lünen 2016, p. xxiii). In Rhythmanalysis, Lefebvre finds rhythm wherever energy is spent in a particular place and time (2004, p. 15). His own example is a Paris junction seen from his window: on red the pedestrians cross, on green the cars rush (p. 28). The light cycles in S3 model that beat. He also holds that where there is rhythm there is measure (p. 8), so counts and timings belong in Situ. I add time on purpose: when places are open and when streets are busy.

Archiving

An archive keeps two things (Schellenberg 1956, pp. 139 to 140). One is the evidence records give of how their maker worked. The other is the information they hold about people and places. Situ keeps both. The city data says what Griffintown is. My decision files and tests record how I turned it into a model. The London Charter, a set of principles for visualising cultural heritage by computer, calls this layer paradata (2009, principle 4.6). The Seville principles ask that a reconstruction show what is real and what is not (López-Menchero & Grande, principle 4), and that others can test it (principle 7). So every decision in my files carries a status, such as confirmed or assumed, and every claim on these pages carries one of four words.

Rhythm is the hard part. Taylor separates the archive of lasting things from the repertoire of embodied practice, like walking to work or lingering at a corner (2003, pp. 19 to 20). The repertoire needs people to be present. Situ cannot store a rhythm the way it stores a street. It can keep traces, such as counts and timings. It can also offer a place where the rhythm is performed again: the game. The Seville principles warn against showing “lifeless cities” (principle 5.3).

What Situ has and what it lacks

Lefebvre describes three moments of social space (1991, pp. 38 to 40). Each kind of source in Situ lines up with one of them, so I use them as a checklist. Every row is open to revision.

conceived space: plans and maps the network, its rules, building types and street objects (S1 to S4, S6), from open data (the city’s and OpenStreetMap’s) and street photos verified

lived space: space as its users live it the game, and what people say about the place, through play and later interviews (From systems to play) planned

These are my own interpretations of the sources. For each one I give the strongest objection I know, and what would show me wrong.

Lefebvre’s triad as a checklist

Lefebvre warns that his three moments lose their force once they become an abstract model (1991, p. 40). Laying them over software comes close to that. So I use the table only to see what is missing, and for now the lived row is empty. The rows also leak: the counts are observation, yet the city decided where to count. If the rows cannot be kept apart at all, the table should show where they cross.

Where layout and movement disagree

A gap between space syntax and observed movement points to something besides the layout, such as a shop or a rule. Griffiths and von Lünen ask research to spell out that kind of gap (2016, p. xxi). The 1993 paper on natural movement (the share of walking the layout alone produces) itself shows shops multiplying the layout’s effect (Hillier et al. 1993, p. 31). A difference may also be plain error, from a wrong network or too few counts. So it counts only after each system’s tests. The reading is wrong if the differences vanish once errors are fixed, or if they follow no pattern.

Schellenberg’s two values

Schellenberg drew his line between evidence and information from government records (1956, pp. 139 to 140, 148). A research model is no government agency. I borrow only the distinction, which he says he defines “quite arbitrarily” (p. 139), and the London Charter already carries it into visualisation. The borrowing fails if the two layers cannot be kept apart. So my corrections live in their own file, and the city’s files stay untouched.

The game as a place to perform rhythm again

Taylor’s repertoire and Lefebvre’s rhythm both need bodies present, and play is embodied action too. Against this, a player alone in a simulation receives no memory from Griffintown’s people. The game itself is code and data, an archive object. Lefebvre adds that the rhythmanalyst is closer to the poet than to “the statistician, who counts things” (2004, p. 23).

I accept most of this. The game keeps traces and sets up a new performance. It does not hold the original repertoire, so interviews and focus groups are in the plan. If players’ movement bears no relation to the recorded rhythms, the game performs nothing of Griffintown.

Space syntax as one measuring stick

Space syntax describes the layout. I use it only to measure, and the simulated walkers never read it. At the end, S9 compares where they went with what space syntax predicts and with the city’s counts. Where they agree, the layout may explain the movement. Where they differ, something else is at work, and that difference is what the archive should keep.

I build Situ as small systems, each with a single job. Each has a blueprint and its own tests. I try it in the Lab before the next one starts.

How I mark what I know

Each claim on these pages, and each system, carries one of four words.

  • verified: the data or a test shows it. A system is verified when it is built and its tests pass.
  • partial: some evidence supports it, and a gap remains. A system is partial when only part of it is built.
  • planned: not built yet.
  • assumption: a reading of mine, or a rule I chose because no source settles it. It may be wrong, and where I can, I say what would show it.

The decision files behind the systems keep their own words, such as confirmed and assumed, because the code reads them.
