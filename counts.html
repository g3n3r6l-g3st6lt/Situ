Movement counts and light timing

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 10, 2026

What the city counted

The city counts traffic at intersections: every car, truck, bus, bicycle and pedestrian, in 15-minute slices. For vehicles it records each movement (northbound left, northbound through and so on). For pedestrians and bicycles it records each crosswalk. The file covers the whole city, 2024 to 2026 (donnees.montreal.ca).

I kept the counts taken within 15 m of an S1 junction. That leaves seven junctions:

Junction Where When Control in S3
J040 Guy, Ottawa and William 3 days, around the clock, December 2024 lights
J051 de la Montagne and Notre-Dame 1 day, 6:30 to 18:15, May 2026 lights
J181 Robert-Bourassa and Saint-Jacques 1 day, 6:30 to 18:15, November 2024 lights, at an exit
J006 Ann and Wellington 1 day, 6:00 to 18:15, February 2024 all-way stop
J056 Murray and Wellington the same day none
J086 Shannon and Wellington the same day stop
J092 Young and Wellington the same day stop

Beyond the lights, these counts are good for two things. S8 will compare simulated movement with space syntax, and these seven junctions let it also compare with movement that was actually counted: what people did on given days. And J040 was counted around the clock for three days, which gives S5 Rhythm a real day to start from.

Why the lights need an estimate

S3 knows where the 31 lights in S1 are. It does not know how long they stay green, because the city does not publish its timing plans. So the timing has to be estimated, and the estimate is mine.

Webster’s method

Webster’s method (Webster 1958) is the classic way to set a fixed light cycle from counted traffic. It asks how full each approach is, and which cycle length keeps the delay smallest. I apply it to the busiest counted hour.

How full an approach is means the cars arriving per hour (a bus or truck counts as two) divided by what its lanes can pass while green, about 1,800 cars an hour per lane. The lanes come from OpenStreetMap. Each phase of the light takes its fullest approach, and the phases add up to a total load. Webster’s formula turns that total, and the time lost each time the green changes (4 s per phase here), into a cycle length. Each phase then gets green in proportion to its load.

I add one check Webster does not make. A pedestrian crossing during a phase needs at least 7 s to start, plus the time to cross at a walking speed of 1.2 m/s. Where the green is shorter, I lengthen it.

The two phases run along Montréal’s north-south, then its east-west. “North” here means the street grid’s north, about 305° on the compass, as in the city’s counts. The counts confirm it: at J040 almost nothing arrives from the north, where Ottawa is the one street no car may arrive along.

What it shows

At J040 (Guy, Ottawa and William), at 16h on 18 December 2024, the total load was 0.29 and Webster’s cycle came to 24 s. At J051 (de la Montagne and Notre-Dame) the load was 0.38 and the cycle 27 s.

The traffic is far from filling these lights. Cycles of 24 and 27 s are much shorter than any city would use, so the cycle I use is my lower limit of 60 s. The green is then set by pedestrians and by the split of loads: 26 s each way at J040, and 38 s north-south against 14 s east-west at J051. That is a finding about Griffintown’s streets, and also a warning: at these volumes, the timing is more a design choice than a consequence of traffic.

J181, on Robert-Bourassa, is skipped. Three of its four approaches lie beyond the network, and Webster needs them all.

The other 29 lights

I weighed four options. One plan for all lights would be simple, but a main street and a side street would get the same green. Borrowing the split of a counted light on the same streets would use real counts, but only two lights were counted. Timing the lights from space syntax was ruled out: S8 compares movement with space syntax, and lights timed from it would make S8 measure its own input. I chose to share the green by road class, which uses the city’s own street hierarchy, already in S1.

Why road class

The city’s 2007 review of its arterial network (PDF) gives each class a daily traffic range: main arteries carry over 30,000 vehicles, collectors 1,000 to 15,000. Managing the flow is “prépondérante” on main arteries and “peu” on collectors (Ville de Montréal 2007). The United States standard says the same: “In general, the higher the traffic volume is, the higher the functional classification will be” (FHWA 2023, p. 26, PDF). And Webster (1958) shares the green in proportion to how full each approach is, so a busier street should get more of it.

Testing it on our counts

At every counted junction where the two streets differ in class, the higher class carried more traffic over the counted day:

Junction Higher class Lower class Ratio
J040 Guy, Ottawa and William William, collector Guy, local 2.5
J006 Ann and Wellington Wellington, secondary arterial Ann, local 8.5
J056 Murray and Wellington Wellington Murray, local 10
J092 Young and Wellington Wellington Young, local 22
J086 Shannon and Wellington Wellington Shannon, local 29

Five out of five, though four are on Wellington, so the sample is small. And road class does not match Webster at the two counted lights. At J040 it would give 21 and 31 s where Webster gives 26 and 26, because at the busiest hour each lane is about as full on both streets. At J051 it would give 26 and 26 s where Webster gives 38 and 14, because the classes are equal but de la Montagne is busier. So the class tells which street gets more green over a day. It cannot tell how much at a given hour. That is why the counted lights keep Webster, and why the share by class is a cautious 60 %.

The rule

Each street arriving at a light belongs to one of the two axes, and each axis takes the highest class among its streets: main arterial (with highway) above secondary arterial, above collector, above local. If both axes have the same class, the two phases share the 52 s of usable green equally. If not, the higher class gets 60 % and the lower 40 %. No phase is shorter than its pedestrians need, and where they need more, the cycle grows. Where cars arrive on one axis only, the other phase is for pedestrians.

Of the 31 lights, 2 follow Webster from counts, 17 have one street of higher class, 7 have equal classes, and 5 have cars on one axis only, mostly at the edge where the cross street lies beyond the network. In S7 these become the light plans, and the 60 % share and the 60 s cycle will be sliders in the Lab.

Assumptions

Every number here is a choice, and two are mine alone. The 60 s lower limit on the cycle (with 120 s as the upper) is my guess for a city centre, and it sets every cycle here. The 60 % share is my own choice: the counts show which street is busier, and say nothing of the amount. The other values (lane capacity, time lost per phase, walking speed and walk interval) follow Webster or common practice; real amber and all-red times are not published, and Montréal does not publish its own walking speed. The lane counts from OpenStreetMap cover 866 of 870 car ways, and I checked them at three junctions only.

The counts themselves are a few days each, in winter or spring. They show those days, not a typical year.

Pedestrian signals

The city’s pedestrian-signal file uses the same intersection numbers as its light file. 30 of the 31 lights in S1 have pedestrian signals. The one without is Seigneurs and William.
