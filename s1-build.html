How S1 is built and tested

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 15, 2026

Blueprint

Rules I assumed

The city publishes the class of each road. Who may use it is my decision, so I wrote it down.

Road class (city’s name) Walk Drive How sure
Local street yes yes safe
Pedestrian way yes no safe
Business place yes no unsure in general; confirmed for Rue Smith (C02)
Collector and arterial yes yes safe
Highway no yes safe
Projected street no no removed: not built yet

Which way cars may go comes from the city’s own one-way code for each street. The road classes come from Ville de Montréal, Géobase, réseau routier (Resources).

The edge

People and cars cross the boundary, so S1 keeps the edge and applies the same rules there as inside, for walkers and for the direction of cars. The tests, and later the agents, assume the rest of the city can be reached from the exits. S3 reads the signs on the edge too, so a junction on the boundary sees every street that meets it. Later systems decide what to do with the edge: S4 puts no buildings there, and S9 measures inside the boundary only.

Numbering

Pieces added by decisions after the October rebuild are numbered after everything else, the edge included. That way, confirming or rejecting one of them never changes another id.

Joining an added path

Each end of an added path joins whatever is nearest within 25 m: a junction, or a street that gets cut at that spot. A junction wins if it is at most 5 m further away. A street dead end within 25 m of the middle of a path is joined to it too. That is how the Allées reach the canal. A decision taken from a photo may instead name the street to join, up to 60 m away.

Two ideas behind it

  • Inside or outside?
  • How far can I get?

To tell whether a point lies inside the study area, imagine a line running right from it and count how often it crosses the outline. An odd count means inside. This is called ray casting. S1 applies it to the halfway point of each segment.

Start at one junction and take every allowed move. Then take every move from each place you reached, and so on. This is called breadth-first search, and each round is one step. The reach view in the Lab plays it round by round.

Tests

Seven checks

Each test asks one question about the network. When the answer is wrong, the test names what failed and where.

Question Answer
T1 Does every segment have a length and two different ends? yes
T2 Can a pedestrian reach every junction? yes
T3 Where are the dead ends? 2, at Rue Dalhousie (C03) and the east end of Rue des Bassins (C09), both confirmed
T4 Can a car get from anywhere to anywhere, obeying one-way streets? yes
T5 Can every segment be traced to its source? yes
T6 Is every street cut at every junction? yes
T7 Do any lines cross without a junction? only where Autoroute 10 passes over Rue de la Commune, confirmed (C01)

T2 and T4 treat the city as connected beyond the exits. T5 matters later, because other systems join their data to S1 through the city’s ids. The Lab shows the latest results.

Reach, in the Lab

T2 and T4 ask their question for every junction at once. To watch one case, open the Lab at Rue Peel and Rue Wellington (J080) and press play. Each step goes one junction further. You can switch between walking and driving, and choose whether the city beyond the exits counts. Click any other junction to start from there.

From J080, a walker reaches all 212 walkable segments in 18 steps. A car reaches 181 of 182 drivable segments without leaving the area. The last one, Boulevard Georges-Vanier (white dashed), can only be reached by leaving Griffintown and coming back in. That comes from where the boundary is drawn. The data is fine.

Steps count junctions. Distances in metres are in the route tool on S1 Streets. Time comes later, when S9 gives its imagined walkers and cars a speed.
