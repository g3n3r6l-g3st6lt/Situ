Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 9, 2026

What the Lab is

The Lab is one map with a tab for each system. There I try a system and see the places it still leaves open. Click anything to see what every system knows about it.

The Lab replays results. It never recomputes them. Every street colour and slider position shows something a system already wrote down: the reach slider, for instance, steps through how far S1 found you can get from a junction. So each rule lives in one place, and the Lab cannot quietly disagree with it.

Routes are the one small exception. Storing every route between every pair of junctions would take many megabytes. So I store only the shortest distances and the last step of each shortest route. The browser follows each route back and tests each street against the detour allowed. Those two rules exist twice, beside the systems and in the browser, and a test runs both on 45,600 cases to check they give the same streets.

What it shows

One system shows at a time, because two colourings on one street cannot be read. Each tab has few controls. It lists its checkpoints by kind, with why each kind matters and the result of its last tests.

Tab What the map shows What you can try
S1 Streets who may use each street, and where gates and dead ends are how far you can get from a junction, how many minutes on foot it takes to reach each street, and every street a trip up to 50 % longer than the shortest could use
S2 Scores choice or integration, on foot or by car a distance slider you can play
S3 Rules stops and lights, or speed limits click a junction, then a street arriving at it, to see its rules and signs
S4 Buildings buildings by use, or by where the use came from the places people go to
S5 Rhythm places open or closed, the counts at the counted junctions, and the bicycle detectors a day, an hour and a mode (and a season for bicycles), and a day that plays
S6 Streetscape trees, hydrants, furniture, lights, cameras, crossings and road works, each with its own icon once you zoom in; sidewalks by material or age one kind of object at a time
S7 Commuters buildings with homes, sized by their commuters, with metro entrances and bus stops one way of travelling at a time
S8 Views how much each street lets you see walk and look: pick two points, and a walker shows what it sees along the way
S9 Observer the morning’s imagined walkers, and the counts inside and around the area a reading and an hour

Integration and choice are the space syntax measures S2 gives each street. Integration is how easy a street is to reach. Choice is how often it lies on the way between other places. The stress tests sit with each system’s tests, where their numbers can be read in full.

How it is built

Each system copies its tables into one file for the Lab. A browser may refuse to read a data file from the disk, yet it always runs a script. So each file is written as a small script, and the Lab works even without a server.

Two orders on one map

Two views put two measures on the same street. In S2, “local or whole” compares a street’s place within 200, 400 or 800 m with its place across the whole island. In S9, “against space syntax” compares a street’s place among the imagined walkers with its place in walking choice or integration. A street’s place runs from 0, the lowest, to 1, the highest; the view shows the first place minus the second. Yellow means higher in the first order, blue higher in the second, grey about the same, and the line widens with the gap. Python works out the gaps (common/ranks.py) and the Lab only draws them; check L13 counts them again a different way.

The gaps describe the data. Why a street is high in one order and low in the other is a question for the pages and for fieldwork (assumption).

Only what is unresolved

Under the map, a button dims everything except what is still open on that tab: checkpoints not yet settled, streets without a value, buildings of unknown use, photo detections not seen recently, streets with no viewpoint. Its label gives the count.

How the Lab is tested

The thirteen Lab checks ask one thing: does the Lab show exactly what the systems wrote?

Question
L1 Do the streets and junctions match S1, and does each junction reach the same streets? Every 15th junction’s reach is worked out again from scratch.
L2 Does every S2 value equal the S2 table, with missing values still missing?
L3 Do the S3 rules, signs, speeds and checkpoints equal the S3 tables?
L4 The Lab no longer shows the S3 stress results, but its data still holds them. Do they give the same counts as the S3 stress tests?
L5 Is every shortest route real and truly the shortest, and do the browser’s route rules give back the original routes?
L6 Does every S4 building and place equal the S4 tables, down to each outline point, with empty cells still empty?
L7 Do the S5 weekday shape, junction counts, each place’s week, the open counts and the ring outside the area equal the S5 tables?
L8 Does every S6 object and surface equal the S6 tables, down to each outline point, with empty cells still empty?
L9 Do the S7 buildings with homes, their commuters and nearest stops, the stops and the hour bands equal the S7 tables?
L10 Do the S8 view measures and kept views equal the S8 tables, and does each sight line the Lab draws land where S8 cast it?
L11 Do the S9 street flows, counted junctions, ring comparison, expectations and ledger equal the S9 tables?
L12 Do the walk tools measure what the systems measure? Sixty walks between random points equal S7’s shortest walks, and thirty views equal S8’s within 2 % (0.04 % on average).
L13 Do the gaps the two-order views show (S2 local or whole, S9 against space syntax) equal a fresh count from the S2 and S9 tables? A street missing a value gets no gap, never 0.

All thirteen pass, and the Lab shows the latest results. Before trusting the checks, I broke the files on purpose. I changed one S2 value in the Lab’s copy, and L2 caught it. Earlier, L3 caught a stop switched off on Rue Chatham. I added 3 m to one walking distance, and L5 found the route no longer added up. When I made the browser’s detour rule one decimetre stricter, L5 found 6,116 cases that differed. For L6, I turned one unknown dwelling count into 0 and moved one outline point by 1 m, and L6 caught both. For L7, I changed one letter of a place’s week, and L7 caught it.

The shortest-route check does not trust the method that found the routes. It uses a simple test instead: if a route is real, and no single move anywhere could shorten any distance, then no shorter route exists. That condition is checked separately, for every start and every move.

In a browser

The checks above read the Lab’s data. Twelve more open the Lab itself in a browser, the way a visitor does, and click through it (last results).

Question
U1 Does every tab open, draw its map, show a key and a text, and mark itself as the one selected?
U2 For every one of the 264 segments, does its panel show exactly the values of S1, S2, S3, S8 and S9, with “none” where a value is missing?
U3 Does every junction open, and do the counted ones show S9’s numbers exactly?
U4 Does a building opened from a link show that building?
U5 Does “Whole area” bring back the first view, and do close, Escape and a new tab empty the panel?
U6 Do the keys say how a missing value is drawn, and does the S2 key name its measure and radius?
U7 When a system’s data fails to load, does its tab say so? When the streets fail, does the Lab say which file?
U8 At phone width, does any page scroll sideways?
U9 With the keyboard alone, can a visitor reach the map, move it and open what lies under the centre cross?
U10 In the two-order views, does each list start with the largest gap in the data, open that street when clicked, and does S9 quote its own correlation?
U11 Does “only what is unresolved” count what is open and dim everything else?
U12 Do the Lab’s headings follow the page outline on every tab, with no level skipped?

All twelve pass. The first run of U8 failed: a table on Tracing a result was wider than a phone. Wide tables now scroll inside themselves.
