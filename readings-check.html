Checking the readings

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 10, 2026

Why this page

Some sentences on this site lean on a reading: a paper says that layout drives movement, a book says which radius is usual. A sentence like that is only as good as the passage behind it. So I set each one beside its passage, with the page, and fixed the ones that said more than the passage does.

How I checked

I turned every PDF in my readings folder into text. The 1993 natural movement paper is a scan, so I read it with OCR (character recognition) first. For each sentence I found the passage, read it in context, noted the printed page and gave a verdict. Where a sentence lacked its passage or page, I added it.

The sentences

Most sentences held. A few said a little more than their reading, and I reworded them; they are marked “partly”.

One more sentence was wrong for another reason. Why I use it still said the city counted at four places on one day. Since October the counts cover seven S1 junctions (Movement counts), and I corrected it.

PlanX against the readings

I also checked the analysis itself against the readings, using PlanX’s own output.

Does PlanX count a journey once, or once each way?

PlanX’s manual left this open: its text halves choice and its formula does not. The output settles it.

A dead-end street segment has one neighbour, and every journey out of the dead end passes through it. When that neighbour meets only two other lines at its far end, nothing else passes through it. Its choice is then simply the number of journeys between the dead end and every other segment. Counted once per pair, that is the number of segments reached less two. Counted once each way, it is double.

For 103 such neighbours, choice equals the once-per-pair count. For none does it equal the once-each-way count. The remaining 18 have other journeys passing through them. So PlanX counts each pair once, as Turner defines it (2007, p. 544).

The readings do not say how Depthmap stores its raw count. If a tool counted both ways, NACH would rise by about 0.05 at radius n here, and about 0.1 at 800 m (likely). That matters before setting Griffintown’s NACH beside a published one.

Is NACH the published formula?

Yes. The published formula gives PlanX’s NACH on all 47,197 lines, at radius n and at 800 m, to the last decimal.

Which NAIN?

Form Source Matches PlanX
NC^1.2 / TD the paper’s text, p. 165 no: median difference 1.3 % at 800 m
(NC + 2)^1.2 / TD the paper’s appendix, p. 191 no: median difference 0.8 % at 800 m
(NC + 2)^1.2 / (TD + 2) PlanX yes, exactly

The order of the segments barely moves between forms (rank correlation 0.9995 at 800 m), so maps and rankings stay the same. The numbers themselves do not travel: compare a NAIN from this site only with one computed by the same form.

Is Griffintown’s network ordinary?

Hillier, Yang and Turner (2012, p. 162) found that the mean NACH of real cities ranges from about 0.7 to 1.2. The whole island of Montréal, on my walking network, has a mean of 0.80 at radius n. That sits inside the range, which suggests the network was built and analysed in an ordinary way.

Readings not used yet

No sentence on the site rests on two readings yet. Offenhuber and Ratti’s Decoding the City (2014) will matter for data and computer vision in the city. The Depthmap 4 handbook and the Depthmap manual 14a will matter when I compare PlanX with that other tool.

The archive readings and Lefebvre’s Rhythmanalysis were added on 10 October and are checked above. The four readings I make of these sources are defended on the About page.

Limits

I checked the sentences against the readings I have, so a claim can be supported here and still be contested elsewhere. OCR can misread a number; I checked the King’s Cross figures against the table layout, and they read consistently. Page numbers are the printed ones, so they match the published versions.
