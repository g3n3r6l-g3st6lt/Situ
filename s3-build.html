How S3 is built and tested

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 15, 2026

Blueprint

Approaches

A sign speaks to drivers arriving from one direction, so S3 starts from the approaches. An approach is one segment end (a segment is one piece of S1 street), seen as a car drives towards the junction. For each one, S3 records the arriving car’s heading, whether the one-way rules let a car arrive or leave along it, the segment’s length, and whether the junction is an exit (where the edge street meets the rest of the city). A one-way street pointing away from a junction gets no rules there, since no car arrives that way.

How a stop sign finds its street

In Québec a stop sign usually stands on the right of the street, just before the junction. S3 relies on that. A post fits an approach when it stands on that same segment, up to 35 m before the junction and 1 to 20 m to the right of the centre line. A post further back belongs to the previous junction. This matters on short pieces, like the 20 m stretches where Wellington splits in two. When several approaches fit, the one closest to the usual spot wins (about 7 m to the right, near the junction), and S3 marks the link as less sure. On a one-way street, signs often stand on both sides, so a post on the left is accepted too. A sign that fits nothing goes on the list to check.

How a turn sign finds its street

Turn signs often stand across the junction or on a median, so their position is a weak clue. S3 adds a second clue: what the sign would mean on each nearby street. At J027, “left turn only” on Rue Chatham keeps the left onto William and removes the right. On Rue William a car can only go straight on, since Chatham is one-way towards William. There the same sign would remove the only move and trap every car. So the signs belong to Chatham.

A turn sign belongs to a street only when it keeps at least one of its moves and removes at least one. When several streets pass this test, the post decides first. Panels on one post usually face the same drivers. So if a turn sign means something on the street of a stop sign on the same post, it goes to that street. Position decides next. A sign before the junction and on the right is preferred. A sign past the junction is used only when nothing before it fits.

Two panels on one post that contradict each other, like “straight only” above “left or right only”, stand back to back. So the second one looks for another street. If none fits, it goes on the list, since only a photo can tell which way each faces. A turn sign at an exit is ignored, because the streets it points to lie outside the network. If the turn signs on one street together leave no move at all, S3 sets them aside and keeps every move open. After the photo checks, no street is in that state.

My first version placed turn signs by position alone, like stop signs, and left many streets with no way out (X5 repeats it).

To name a turn, S3 compares the arriving heading with the direction of the street the car leaves along. Less than 30° either way counts as straight on. Anything sharper is a left or a right (Chatham onto William turns 89° to the left). S3 never makes a U-turn.

Decisions from photos

For the few signs I could not place from the desk, I wrote down what Mapillary’s street-level photos show (S3 checks with photos). S3 reads those decisions before its own matching, so a photo outranks a guess. The city’s file is never edited. Removing a decision brings back S3’s own matching.

No-entry signs

S1 already takes its one-way streets from the city, so S3 uses no-entry signs only to check S1. A sign agrees when a street near it is one-way towards the junction, or closed to cars. When only a path I added in S1 explains a sign, it goes on the list, because my own decision cannot confirm itself.

Traffic lights

The city’s light file gives each light a point and the names of the streets it stands between. S3 gives each light to the closest junction within 25 m. A light 25 to 100 m from every junction goes on the list, since it may mark a junction S1 is missing. The names give a second check: “Montagne / Wellington” should stand where Rue de la Montagne meets Rue Wellington.

Speed limits

The city’s speed file uses the same street ids as S1, so most segments simply take their own limit. Seven segments have a limit of 0. All seven are closed to cars, so S3 reads 0 as “no limit given”. Three Wellington pieces were redrawn after the file was made and have new ids. For those, S3 takes the speed of lines with the same name lying along the segment within 5 m, when they cover nearly all of it and agree. I chose 5 m by looking at those three segments. It is the smallest distance that covers the two short pieces where Wellington splits. A stress test shows what other distances give.

Assumptions

These are my choices for the simulation. None of them comes from the data.

Assumption Why Where it could be wrong
stop signs stand on the right the usual placement in Québec a median or a wide corner
a turn sign must mean something on its street signs are put up to change what drivers do a sign that really is redundant on the ground
panels on one post face the same drivers, unless they contradict the usual way posts are dressed a post serving two streets without contradiction
a photo decision outranks the matching the photo shows the street; the matching guesses a photo older than its sign (why P03 stays open)
no U-turns no data on where drivers make them a driver who missed a turn
time-limited turn rules hold at all hours S3 has no clock until S5 Rhythm rush-hour bans on 3 streets
temporary lights count as lights the city lists them with the others Notre-Dame and Murray (J059), if removed
a new street piece keeps the limit of the old line beneath it same street, same name the three redrawn Wellington pieces

Tests

Ten checks

Each check asks one plain question of the rules S3 writes. Eight must pass, and all eight do. The other two only report. The Lab shows the latest results.

Question Answer
T1 Does every street a car can arrive along have exactly one approach? yes
T2 Does every sign get a street, or a written reason it has none? yes
T3 Does every stop sign stand where a driver would see it? yes
T4 Do the turns fit the street layout, with a reason for every forbidden turn and no street left a trap? yes
T5 Does each junction’s control agree with its approaches? yes
T6 Do the no-entry signs agree with the city’s one-way streets? report: none of the 78 disagrees, and 67 agree
T7 Does every rule cite the signs behind it? yes
T8 How sure is each link between a sign and a street? report: see S3 Rules
T9 Does every light stand within 25 m of its junction and name at least one of its streets? yes
T10 Does every speed match the city file, with a reason for every gap? yes

A disagreement in T6 goes on the list of places to check, because I cannot tell from the desk which source is right. The photos explained the last four.

TipTesting the tests

A test that always passes proves nothing. Before trusting T9 and T10, I broke the data on purpose. I moved one light by 30 m and changed two speed cells: one from 30 to 50, and one empty cell to 0. T9 caught the light and T10 caught both speeds. Then I put the files back.

Stress tests

Each stress test changes one thing on purpose and counts how many rules move. Run twice on the same input (X1), S3 gives identical results.

X2 moves every sign post at random, by up to 1, 2, 4 and 8 m. Up to 2 m, almost nothing changes. At 4 m, ten streets change their stop, and at 8 m, 28 do. So the city’s sign positions would have to be off by more than 2 m to shift the stop rules. I do not know yet how accurate they are, and a few corners are worth checking on the ground.

X3 and X4 change how far before the junction, and how far to the right, a sign may stand. Both sit on a plateau, a range where small changes do nothing: a little more or less than my 35 m and 20 m changes no rule. Only the tightest values lose signs. Most signs without a street stand at exits or beyond the edge.

X5 places turn signs by position alone, as my first version did. It leaves 25 streets with no turn at all. With the meaning test and the photo decisions, no street is left that way.

X6 changes how close a light must be to its junction. From 25 to 40 m, the same 31 junctions get lights. At 5 m, eight are lost, mostly on Robert-Bourassa and on edge streets. There the city places the light 5 to 10 m away on a wide corner. At 80 m, lights start landing on the wrong junctions.

X7 changes how far a same-name line may lie when a street is missing from the speed file. It is the one setting with no plateau below it. Under 5 m, two Wellington pieces open to cars lose their limit. From 5 m on, all three redrawn pieces are covered, which is why I chose 5 m (speed limits). Autoroute 10 and its ramps are never in the file, at any distance.
