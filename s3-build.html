How S3 is built

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 10, 2026

Blueprint

The problem

The city’s sign file gives each post a position and a list of panels. It does not say which street a sign faces. A stop sign at a corner stands close to two or three streets at once, and only one of them has to stop. So for each sign, S3 has to work out which street it was put there for.

Approaches

A sign speaks to the drivers coming from one direction. So S3 first lists every approach: each end of each street segment, seen as a car drives towards the junction. For each approach it notes four things: the direction a car is heading as it arrives, whether the one-way rules let a car arrive or leave along it, how long the segment is, and whether the junction is an exit, where the edge street meets the rest of the city.

The end of a one-way street pointing away from the junction gets no rules, since no car arrives that way.

How a stop sign finds its street

In Québec a stop sign usually stands on the right of the street, just before the junction. S3 relies on that.

For each post, S3 looks at the approaches nearby and measures two distances: how far before the junction the post stands, and how far to the right of the street’s centre line. A post fits an approach when it stands up to 35 m before the junction and between 1 and 20 m to the right. It must also stand on that segment: a post further back belongs to the previous junction. This matters on very short pieces, like the 20 m stretches where Wellington splits in two.

If several approaches fit, the one closest to the usual spot wins (about 7 m to the right, near the junction), and S3 marks the link as less sure. On a one-way street signs often stand on both sides, so a post on the left is accepted too. If nothing fits, the sign gets no street and goes on the list to check.

How a turn sign finds its street

Turn signs are harder. They often stand across the junction or on a median, so where a turn sign stands is a weak clue. S3 adds a second clue: what the sign would mean on each street nearby.

Back at J027, both signs say “left turn only”, and two streets are close enough to own them. On Rue Chatham a car can turn left or right onto William, so “left only” keeps the left and removes the right. That makes sense. On Rue William, from either side, a car can only go straight on, since Chatham is one-way towards William. There “left only” would remove the only move and trap every car. So the signs belong to Chatham.

The rule is this: a turn sign belongs to a street only when it keeps at least one move the street has and removes at least one. If several streets pass, two things decide. First, panels on one post usually face the same drivers, so if the post also carries a stop sign and the turn sign means something on that street, it goes there. Then position: a sign before the junction, on the right, is preferred, and a sign past the junction is used only when nothing before it fits.

Two panels on one post that contradict each other, like “straight only” above “left or right only”, cannot face the same drivers. They stand back to back, so the second one looks for another street. If there is none, it gets no street and a place on the list to check. Only a photo can tell which way each one faces.

A turn sign at an exit is ignored, because the streets it points to lie outside the network. If several turn signs on one street together leave no move at all, S3 sets them aside and keeps every move open. After the photos, no street is in that state.

My first version placed turn signs by position alone, like stop signs, and left many streets with no way out. One of the stress tests repeats that version so the difference stays visible.

To name a turn, S3 compares the direction a car is heading as it arrives with the direction of the street it leaves along. Less than 30° either way counts as straight on. Anything sharper is a left or a right. From Chatham onto William the heading turns 89° to the left. S3 never makes a U-turn.

Decisions from photos

A few signs could not be placed from the desk. For those I looked at Mapillary’s photos and wrote down what I saw (the photo checks). S3 reads those decisions before its own matching, so a photo outranks a guess. The city’s file is never edited, so removing a decision brings back S3’s own matching.

No-entry signs

A no-entry sign stands at the mouth of a street a car may not enter. S1 already knows the one-way streets from the city, so S3 uses these signs only to check S1. A sign agrees when a street near it is one-way towards the junction, or closed to cars. If only a path I added in S1 explains it, it goes on the list to check, because my own decision cannot confirm itself.

Traffic lights

The city’s light file gives each light a point and the names of the streets it stands between. S3 gives each light to the closest junction within 25 m. A light between 25 and 100 m from every junction goes on the list to check, because it may point to a junction S1 is missing.

The street names give a second check: “Montagne / Wellington” should stand where Rue de la Montagne meets Rue Wellington. The file says nothing about timing, so that belongs to S7, as an assumption.

Speed limits

The city’s speed file uses the same street ids as S1, so most segments simply take the limit written on their own line.

Two things need care. Seven segments have a limit of 0 in the file, and all seven are closed to cars, so S3 reads 0 as “no limit given” and leaves the limit empty. And three Wellington pieces were redrawn after the speed file was made, so they have new ids. For those, S3 looks for lines of the same street name lying along the segment, within 5 m. If they cover nearly all of it and agree on one speed, S3 takes that speed.

The 5 m is the smallest distance that covers the two short pieces where Wellington splits. I chose it by looking at those three segments, and a stress test shows what other distances would give.

Assumptions

These are my choices for the simulation. None of them comes from the data.

Assumption Why Where it could be wrong
stop signs stand on the right the usual placement in Québec a sign on a median or a wide corner
a turn sign must mean something on its street a sign is put up to change what drivers do a sign that is truly redundant on the ground
panels on one post face the same drivers, unless they contradict the usual way posts are dressed a post with panels for two streets that do not contradict
a photo decision outranks the matching the photo shows the street; the matching only guesses a photo older than the sign it shows (P03 stays open for that reason)
no U-turns I have no data on where drivers make them a driver who missed a turn
time-limited turn rules hold at all hours S3 has no clock yet; S5 Rhythm will give one rush-hour bans on 3 streets
temporary lights count as lights the city file lists them with the others the lights at Notre-Dame and Murray (J059), if they have been removed
a new street piece keeps the limit of the old line beneath it the street and its name are the same the three redrawn pieces of Wellington
