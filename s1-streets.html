S1 Streets

What S1 does

S1 turns the city’s street lines into a network of segments and junctions, and records who may use each segment. Every later system walks or drives on this network, so it has to be right first. The rules I assumed decide who may walk or drive where. I check the result twice: against itself in the tests, and against the street, using photos, in Checks and decisions.

Words

A segment is a piece of street between two junctions. A junction is where segment ends meet. Griffintown is part of the city, so S1 also keeps the streets just outside the boundary that touch the study area. I call them the edge. A gate is where the study area meets the edge. An exit is the far end of an edge street, where the city carries on. A dead end is a junction with only one segment that is neither a gate nor an exit. A one-way arrow shows the only direction cars may use; pedestrians may always walk both ways. A checkpoint, with an id starting with C, is a place where I doubted the data and decided from photos.

S1 starts from the city’s street lines, the same lines my space syntax analysis uses. Space syntax, begun by Bill Hillier and Julienne Hanson, describes where each street sits in the whole network. S2 attaches its values to S1. Every segment keeps the city’s own id, so any value can be traced back to its source.

Result

232 segments inside · 32 on the edge

Counts: S1 segments, the pieces of street between two junctions, inside the study area and on its edge. They come from the city’s Géobase as cut in Sample 4, with the paths and the decisions I checked on photos (Checks and decisions).

Does not show: how long or how busy the streets are. The number depends on where the junctions fall.

Before the checks, S1 had 190 segments and 12 dead ends. Now it has 2 dead ends. A road map is a poor walking map. The city’s file left out the canal path and the new park paths, and 10 of the 12 dead ends turned out to be places where walkers continue.

What changed in October

The October rebuild came from decisions about the boundary and a few street ends. Every segment and junction that existed before kept its number.

The boundary was a drawing decision. The city draws Boulevard Robert-Bourassa as two one-way roads about 60 m apart. The boundary ran between them, so inside the study area the boulevard carried traffic one way only. S1 now takes in both carriageways (C19) and keeps the edge around the whole area.

At the street ends, the Lachine Canal path (C16) and two park paths (C18) came in for walkers. A footway now runs from the end of Brennan through the block to de la Commune (C04, C12), and the end of Ann joins it (C05). These links are assumed. They stay pink until a photo or a visit confirms them. From the end of Smith, a 41 m link crosses the plaza of Place des Aiguilleurs (C06). Photos settled that one.

From here to there

In the Lab, choose S1, then “from here to there”, and click a start and an end junction. Everything it shows comes from the network alone:

Measure What it tells you
Shortest route the shortest way along the streets, on foot or by car; the other measures compare against it
Directness route length divided by the straight line; a grid gives about 1.3, and a barrier such as the canal pushes it higher
Variety in blue, every street a trip could use if it were at most 0, 5, 10, 25 or 50 % longer than the shortest; a thin band means the layout funnels everyone onto one street
Cost of one-way streets how much longer the drive is than the walk between the same two junctions

Choose “minutes on foot” and click anywhere near a street to see how far you can walk from there. Streets you reach within 5 minutes turn yellow, within 10 blue and within 15 violet, at an ordinary pace of 1.3 m a second; many people walk slower. The side lists the homes, places and stops in reach. Only the streets of the area are walked, so a place just past its edge may be closer than it looks.

From William at Young to further along William is 306 m on foot and 966 m by car. William is one-way, so the car has to go round the block. From Bassins at Rioux to Notre-Dame at Robert-Bourassa, a 10 % detour opens up 82 other segments: the grid gives walkers many ways. From Bassins at Allée des Barges to Wellington, the walk is 1.7 times the straight line, because the canal has few crossings.

These measures say nothing about how anyone actually chooses a route. People do not all take the shortest way, and the turn bans and stops of S3 are left out. Routes also stay inside the study area, so many car trips would need the city outside. The Lab says so when that happens. How people choose belongs to S9, which can measure any route it imagines against this yardstick. The Lab notes describe how the measures themselves are tested.
