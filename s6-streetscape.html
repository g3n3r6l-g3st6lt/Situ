S6 Streetscape

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 16, 2026

What S6 does

S6 records what stands on Griffintown’s streets besides the buildings: trees, fire hydrants, benches and other furniture, street lights, surveillance cameras, pedestrian crossings, the sidewalks and traffic islands themselves, and the road works under way on the day I saved the city’s list. Each object keeps its source and the values that source gives, and is linked to the S1 street it stands on.

S6 decides nothing about what these objects do. A tree may shade a walker or hide a shop; a hydrant means no car may stop beside it. Those effects are assumptions, so they belong to S9, which will write each one down. What a walker can see from where they stand comes from the system after S7, built from the S4 buildings and these trees.

What it found

971 trees · 104 hydrants · 806 street lights · 487 sidewalk pieces · 29 road works

Counts: objects inside the study area. Trees, hydrants, sidewalks and road works come from the city’s files saved on 16 October 2026. Street lights come from OpenStreetMap and from Mapillary’s photo detections, each light counted once.

Does not show: everything that stands there. Trees on private land are missing, and the detections miss what no photo shows.

The streetscape is young (verified, for what the city records). Of the 775 public trees with a planting date, 651 were planted in 2013 or later, and three quarters of all trunks are less than 18 cm across. Of the 267 sidewalk pieces with a construction date, 175 were built in 2020 or later. The commonest species are elms, honey-locusts, poplars and Kentucky coffeetrees.

My reading, and it is an interpretation: Griffintown’s street furniture is as new as its towers, and its trees are still small. A view along a street today is probably more open than it will be in twenty years. Any study of what can be seen here describes one moment in a growing place.

All 286 pieces of furniture, from benches and bicycle racks to drinking fountains, come from OpenStreetMap, since the city’s furniture file covers only its large parks. Mappers may have missed some (assumption). On 16 October 2026 the city listed 29 road works in the area, from sidewalk rebuilding on Saint-Martin to construction on Young that runs until June 2027.

Read by a machine

Street lights

The city publishes no file of its street lights (verified, for its open data catalogue). OpenStreetMap records 72 in the area. Mapillary is a site where people and the city upload street photos. Its software scans every photo for objects, street lights among them, and places each one on the map. It found street lights in 888 places here. Detections less than 3 m apart are joined into one light, since a pole often carries two heads, which leaves 764. Counting each light found by either source once, the area has 806.

The hydrants show how far the machine can be trusted, since the city’s file lists every one. Mapillary’s software found 82 hydrants, and 68 of them stand within 5 m of one of the city’s, half of them less than a metre away. So it found 68 of the city’s 104. Where its photos reach, it is mostly right and well placed, and it misses about a third (partial; some misses may be streets no photo covers).

The two sources of lights barely meet. Only 30 of OpenStreetMap’s 72 lamps have a detection within 5 m. Most of the others line the east end of Rue des Bassins and Allée des Barges, where the sheet shows almost no detections. Those are walking streets beside the basins, and most photos are taken from cars (assumption). The machine sees what the camera passed, so a walker’s street may be the one it knows least.

Crossings and cameras

The same software finds surveillance cameras and the paint of pedestrian crossings, and OpenStreetMap records both. The two agree on crossings, and barely on cameras.

Crossings are where the machine and the mappers meet. Of the 102 painted crossings Mapillary’s software found, 74 stand within 5 m of a crossing OpenStreetMap records as painted or signalled, half of them less than 2 m away. OpenStreetMap records 481 crossings, 289 of them unmarked, and the photos find 83 of its 192 painted ones. Six painted crossings in the photos stand where OpenStreetMap says there is no paint: either a crossing painted since, or a tag nobody updated (assumption).

The cameras barely overlap. The photos show 34 and OpenStreetMap has 55, with only 2 in both. Most of OpenStreetMap’s cameras are tagged as private outdoor cameras along Wellington, Young and Murray, probably mapped by someone reading building fronts (assumption). A few are the city’s traffic cameras at corners of Peel and de la Montagne. The software’s cameras spread across the area, six of them on Rue William. Each source finds a different set of watchers, and neither finds them all.

The software also finds poles, and S6 leaves them out. The street lights already show which streets are lit, and the number of poles depends mostly on how close two detections must be to count as one.

Street furniture

For benches and bicycle racks, the machine and the mappers agree too little for either to fill the other’s gaps. Bins are the same: Mapillary’s software finds 320 where OpenStreetMap has 38, and many are probably household bins set out on collection day (assumption). S6 keeps those detections out of its objects and reports the comparison in its tests.

What S6 leaves out

The city’s tree inventory covers public trees only, so trees in courtyards and on private land are missing. The city’s canopy model and its aerial LiDAR, a laser scan from the air, date from 2015, before most of today’s towers and trees, so S6 does not use them. The city’s art data holds a single subsidized mural in the area and none of its own public art collection. The artworks OpenStreetMap records are already among the S4 places. The city publishes no file of its street lights, so S6 takes them from OpenStreetMap and from photos.

Checkpoints

Checkpoints are cases set aside for a closer look. Five road works are still listed although their permits ended the day before I saved the file (K01 to K05). They may have been extended, or the list may lag behind the street. Thirty-five objects were found only in photos taken before 2023 (K06 to K40): 26 street lights, 6 painted crossings and 3 cameras. Griffintown has been rebuilt street by street since then, and some may be gone.

In the Lab

In the Lab, the S6 tab draws the sidewalks under the streets, coloured by material or by the year they were built. Every tree, hydrant, piece of furniture, light, camera, crossing and road work sits on top, as a coloured dot from afar and as its own small icon once you zoom in. Click anything to see its source and values. How S6 is built gives the rules and the tests.
