S7 Walkers

Author

Mohamed Gharbia

Published

October 16, 2026

What S7 does

S7 says who walks where and when on an ordinary weekday. It starts with the two groups the census describes: the people who live here and go out to work, and the people who come here to work. For each building with homes and each workplace, it estimates how many people walk, at what hour, to which stop or street, and along which S1 streets.

S7 reads neither the city’s counts nor space syntax. Both stay out so that S9 can compare its walkers with them. Visitors to shops and cafés, children going to school and walks taken for their own sake are not here yet. They will come as layers of their own, each with its assumptions written down. What a walker can see along the way, the perception part of S7, comes after them.

What it found

about 4,900 people walk a weekday commute · peak at 8h, again at 17h

The 11,954 homes in S4 send about 7,200 residents out to work on a weekday, and about 4,600 people come here to work (likely, for the reasons under What it rests on). Of the residents who commute, about a quarter walk the whole way, and about a fifth walk to the metro or a bus. Most of the people who work here come by car, so only a third of them walk on Griffintown’s streets at all, from a metro entrance or a bus stop.

Two streets carry the commute: Peel and de la Montagne, which lead north towards downtown and towards the Bonaventure and Lucien-L’Allier metro stations. On one stretch of Peel, about 290 walkers pass between 8 and 9h. Bonaventure takes about 1,900 walks a day, Lucien-L’Allier about 1,500 and Georges-Vanier, to the west, about 500.

My reading, and it is an interpretation: the commute uses Griffintown as a passage to downtown, along its north-south streets. The Lachine Canal path carries no commuter at all in S7, so whatever life it has on a weekday comes from walks S7 does not hold yet. That gap is itself something to compare later with the counts and with what people say about the place.

What it rests on

The census of May 2021 is the only open source that says, tract by tract, when people leave for work, when they arrive and how they travel (certain). Its week fell during the pandemic, and 42 to 56 % of the employed residents of the three tracts worked from home. Across Canada, working from home fell from 24 % in that week to 17 % in May 2025. S7 applies that fall to each tract once, so a tract where 55 % worked from home now counts about 39 %. That is my one correction, and every number of walkers moves with it; the stress test shows by how much.

The census also probably undercounts transit and walking today: in 2021 people avoided buses and the metro, and the regional travel survey of 2023 shows walking up 6 points in central Montréal since 2018 (likely). So S7 probably shows too few walkers, and I would rather say so than guess a second correction. The 2026 census should replace all of this, but its commuting results will likely come out after this prototype ends.

The number of homes is today’s, from the tax roll in S4, so the towers built since 2021 are counted. Where they go and which streets they take are assumptions, set out in How S7 is built.

Checkpoints

The census places about 44 workers in a corner of a fourth tract inside the area, where S4 lists no business premises (K01). S7 cannot place them yet.

In the Lab

In the Lab, the S7 tab draws every street as thick as the walkers on it in the chosen hour. Play the day to watch the morning and evening commutes, or show only residents or only the people who work here. Click a street for its walkers by group.
